# Design Taste

Taste comes from a specific point of view, disciplined editing, and fidelity to the subject.

## Establish a visual thesis

Write one internal sentence that links the subject to the form.

Examples:
- A hospital automation architecture rendered like a calm clinical systems map.
- A developer-tool PRD presented as a precise field manual with runnable-feeling examples.
- A housing research report treated as an editorial dossier with evidence pinned to each conclusion.

The thesis should affect typography, rhythm, motifs, diagram style, and interaction.

## Choose a direction, not a theme pack

Possible directions:
- editorial and authoritative
- technical and instrument-like
- archival and documentarian
- warm and human-centered
- utilitarian and operations-focused
- cinematic and narrative
- restrained luxury
- playful but rigorous
- dense professional reference

Do not mix unrelated directions.

## Typography

- Use system font stacks by default for portability.
- Create personality through scale, width, weight, casing, spacing, and layout before importing fonts.
- Keep body text readable, typically 16–19px with a line-height around 1.45–1.7.
- Keep long-form measure near 55–78 characters.
- Use display type where it communicates the thesis, not throughout the page.
- Avoid defaulting to Inter-like neutral SaaS typography for every subject.
- Use monospace for identifiers, code, timestamps, or machine-oriented labels, not as decoration.

## Color

Start with semantics:
- base canvas
- primary ink
- secondary ink
- subtle surface
- rule or boundary
- accent
- success, warning, danger, information

Use accent color as punctuation, not wallpaper. Ensure contrast. Do not rely on color alone for meaning.

## Layout

- Create one dominant reading path.
- Allow asymmetry when it reflects hierarchy.
- Mix wide overview sections with narrower reading sections.
- Use sticky navigation only for genuinely long artifacts.
- Let dense material be dense when precision matters, but manage it with alignment and grouping.
- Avoid filling every gap.

## Shape and surface

- Pick one radius philosophy: square, slight, or soft.
- Use shadows only when elevation or overlay matters.
- Borders are often better than shadows for document-like artifacts.
- Avoid nested rounded cards.
- Avoid glassmorphism unless the subject strongly justifies it.
- Avoid gradient backgrounds as an automatic signal of “premium.”

## Imagery and diagrams

Prefer:
- diagrams derived from actual relationships
- small abstract motifs tied to the subject
- meaningful icons
- source imagery supplied by the user
- inline SVG with a consistent visual language

Avoid:
- generic stock imagery
- random decorative blobs
- unlabeled abstract charts
- icons used as filler

## Motion

One orchestrated moment is stronger than many unrelated effects.

Good uses:
- reveal the system flow in sequence
- highlight a selected comparison criterion
- preserve context while navigating
- show change between states

Always support reduced motion. Never delay access to content for animation.

## The aesthetic risk

Take one controlled risk:
- an unusual but readable headline treatment
- a subject-specific navigation model
- a distinctive diagram language
- a strong asymmetrical composition
- an interactive micro-tool
- a restrained monochrome palette with one semantic accent

The risk must improve identity or understanding.

## Anti-slop review

Before finalizing, ask:
- Could this page belong to any startup?
- Did I use cards because the content required them?
- Is the hero a thesis or a template?
- Does every accent encode something?
- Did I make one memorable choice?
- Did I remove at least one unnecessary visual device?
- Would the artifact remain strong in grayscale?
